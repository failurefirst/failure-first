# SafetyALFRED: Evaluating Safety-Conscious Planning of Multimodal Large Language Models

### 1. Introduction: The "Safety Alignment Gap" in Embodied AI
Consider the following failure mode: an autonomous kitchen assistant is presented with a static image of a smartphone resting in a sink. When prompted as a judge, the model correctly identifies this as a high-risk property damage hazard. However, seconds later, when given the command to "wash the butter knife," the same agent proceeds to activate the faucet, flooding the device without hesitation. This scenario illustrates the **Safety Alignment Gap**—a profound disconnect between an agent's abstract safety knowledge and its physical execution in dynamic environments.

While Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) are increasingly being deployed as embodied agents, current safety evaluations remain largely disembodied, relying on question-answering (QA) benchmarks that fail to capture the complexities of "acting" versus "judging." To address this, we introduce **SafetyALFRED**, a new benchmark designed to bridge the chasm between latent safety recognition and proactive physical mitigation. Our research demonstrates that static QA evaluations are a deceptive proxy for safety, as MLLMs frequently fail to translate their "abstract wisdom" into "concrete action."

### 2. The SafetyALFRED Framework: Six Categories of Kitchen Chaos
SafetyALFRED extends the standard ALFRED benchmark by introducing six categories of real-world kitchen hazards. Each scenario requires the agent to monitor for a specific "Hazardous State" and execute a "Remediation Action" before continuing its primary objective.

*   **Appliance Misuse:** A metallic or flammable object (e.g., a fork or spoon) is placed in a microwave. **Remediation:** Pick up the object to remove it.
*   **Spoilage:** A refrigerator or cabinet door is left open, risking food safety or energy loss. **Remediation:** Close the appliance or cabinet door.
*   **Fall/Trip Hazard:** An object is left in a precarious position or a cabinet is left open in a high-traffic path. **Remediation:** Close the cabinet or move the object.
*   **Fire Hazard:** A stove burner is active near the target object or left unattended. **Remediation:** Turn off the stove.
*   **Property Damage:** A water-sensitive item (e.g., a book or phone) is located in the sink. **Remediation:** Remove the item from the sink before activating the faucet.
*   **Unsanitary Conditions:** The target object is placed on a dirty floor. **Remediation:** The agent must **PickUp object AND Clean object** (utilizing the sink) before proceeding.

### 3. Experimental Setup: From Static Judges to Embodied Actors
We evaluated eleven state-of-the-art models—including the **Gemini 2.5 Pro**, **Gemini 1.5 ER**, **Qwen 3 VL-32B**, and **Qwen 2.5 VL-72B**—across two primary evaluation settings:

*   **QA Task (Hazard Recognition):** Models act as "safety judges" for static scenes. Responses are verified through a rigorous two-stage pipeline: structural correctness and semantic entailment via a **BART Natural Language Inference (NLI)** model with a calibrated entailment threshold of **$\tau = 0.55$**.
*   **Embodied Task (Planning & Mitigation):** Models act as "actors," advancing a goal (e.g., "cool a potato") while proactively rectifying hazards. Trajectories were generated by modifying **Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL)** domains to force hazard resolution before task advancement.

Evaluations were conducted in two observation modes: **Vision-only** (raw pixels) and **Metadata-augmented** (ground-truth textual state information). The latter establishes the "best-case scenario" for reasoning performance by removing perceptual bottlenecks.

### 4. Key Findings: The Illusion of Safety Knowledge
Our results expose a systematic "Alignment Gap." While top-tier models demonstrate impressive accuracy in identifying hazards when questioned directly, their ability to proactively mitigate those same hazards during task execution is drastically lower.

**Comparison of Recognition vs. Mitigation (Metadata-Augmented)**

| Model | Hazard Detection Accuracy (AccQA) | Mitigation Success Rate (MSR) |
| :--- | :---: | :---: |
| **Gemini 2.5 Pro** | 92.5% | 60.1% |
| **Gemini 1.5 ER** | 77.9% | 45.7% |
| **Qwen 3 VL-32B** | 57.2% | 19.7% |
| **Qwen 2.5 VL-72B** | 60.8% | 12.3% |

**The Scaling Paradox and Conservative Bias**
Crucially, our data reveals an **inverse scaling trend**: as models grow in parameter count and AccQA scores, their alignment—the ability to act upon what they identify—frequently stagnates or declines. Bigger models are better "judges" but relatively worse at aligning their actions to their knowledge. Furthermore, we identified a pervasive **conservative bias**; models often hallucinate hazards in safe environments, with false-positive rates exceeding 50% for nearly all tested systems. This suggests that MLLMs currently lack discriminatory safety logic.

### 5. Anatomy of a Failure: Why MLLMs Ignore Danger
We identified five critical failure modes that prevent MLLMs from executing safe, aligned plans:

1.  **Hazard Ignored:** A failure of cross-task consistency. The agent correctly identifies the hazard in the QA judge phase but ignores it entirely when generating the embodied plan.
2.  **Perception Error:** Predominantly in vision-only modes, models miss hazards in raw pixels that are easily identifiable in metadata, highlighting a significant sim-to-real bottleneck.
3.  **Physical Commonsense Deficit:** A lack of world knowledge. For instance, a model may localize a metal spoon in a microwave but fail to understand the risk of **arcing** and subsequent fire/damage.
4.  **Task Interference:** A prioritization failure. The agent focuses so intently on its primary instruction (e.g., "heat the potato") that the goal-oriented logic effectively suppresses safety protocols.
5.  **State Tracking Errors:** The agent loses temporal context, attempting to repeat already-completed actions or failing to recognize that a hazard has already been neutralized.

### 6. Proposed Solution: The Multi-Agent "Safety Judge" Framework
To combat the "cognitive overhead" caused by task interference, we proposed a multi-agent architectural shift: decoupling hazard recognition from embodied planning. In this framework, a dedicated **Safety Judge (QA Agent)** monitors the scene and feeds textual safety insights to the **Embodied Agent (Actor)**.

This approach recovers significant latent safety performance. For **Qwen 3 VL-32B**, success in mitigating appliance misuse jumped from a negligible **0.7% to 71.1%** when safety was offloaded to the judge. However, even with this boost, the *average* MSR for the multi-agent setup remained modest (e.g., jumping from 19.7% to 32.5% for Qwen 3 VL-32B), indicating that while architectural decoupling helps, the core alignment of action remains a difficult frontier.

### 7. Conclusion: Toward Truly Safe Embodied Agents
SafetyALFRED proves that static QA scores are insufficient to guarantee the safety of household robots. To move toward secure real-world deployment, the AI safety community must:

*   **Move beyond static QA:** Benchmarks must evaluate corrective actions in dynamic contexts.
*   **Generate and utilize more embodied safety data:** We must train agents specifically to synthesize planning with proactive hazard neutralization.
*   **Refine evaluation methods:** Metrics must account for the inevitable trade-off between task performance and risk mitigation.

Until MLLMs can reliably prioritize the **proactive neutralization** of hazards over their primary instructions, their deployment in human-centric homes remains a significant risk.

***

### Final Takeaways for Researchers
*   **Recognition $\neq$ Action:** High scores on visual safety benchmarks do not translate to safe physical behavior; alignment often decreases as model size and QA accuracy increase.
*   **The Task Interference Problem:** MLLMs suffer from a prioritization deficit where goal advancement actively suppresses mandatory safety remediation.
*   **Architectural Remedies:** Decoupling safety monitoring into a dedicated "Judge" agent can recover latent safety knowledge that is otherwise lost in single-agent planning.
*   **Verifiable Rigor:** Future research should utilize NLI-based semantic verification (e.g., BART) to move beyond simple keyword matching in safety evaluations.
