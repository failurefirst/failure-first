## 1. The Dynamic Wind Hazard: Why Deterministic UAV Routing Fails

In air-ground autonomous delivery systems, mobile ground vehicles provide long-range transport and payload capacity, while unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) are deployed from the vehicle platform to serve spatially dispersed customers. However, low-altitude aerial delivery operations face a critical safety hazard: irreversible battery depletion failures caused by dynamic environmental wind. Deterministic or static energy models assume constant power consumption over planned flight paths. In real-world low-altitude environments, these static assumptions underestimate the cumulative impact of headwinds, crosswinds, dynamic aerodynamic drag, and battery-voltage drops. This systematic underestimation frequently leads to return-feasibility failures, where a UAV exhausts its battery energy before successfully returning to the mobile truck or central depot.

A primary driver of these airborne energy depletion failures is the imperfect nature of online wind observations. Onboard wind estimates are inherently delayed by a sensing and update offset $\tau_e$ and corrupted by noise resulting from hardware limitations, multi-rotor body vibrations, and low sensor sampling frequencies. Standard reactive replanning algorithms—such as dynamic shortest-path methods or standard online replanners—frequently fail under these conditions because they select forward paths based on current point-estimates of wind speed and direction without explicitly verifying whether the platform retains sufficient energy reserves to safely return to the recovery vehicle under worst-case estimation errors.

To eliminate these return-feasibility risks under unmodeled wind uncertainty, Tianshun Li et al. (2026) introduced the Energy-Aware Wind-Resilient Routing (EWR) framework in their paper, *"Energy-Aware Wind-Resilient Routing for Truck-Assisted Multi-UAV Delivery under Wind Uncertainty"*. The EWR framework replaces deterministic energy assumptions with an online, risk-sensitive planning architecture engineered to guarantee safe return feasibility under partially observable, time-varying wind fields.

## 2. Architecture of the Energy-Aware Wind-Resilient Routing (EWR) Framework

### 2.1 System Architecture & Layer Breakdown

The EWR framework operates via a three-layer execution pipeline that integrates offline allocation with online risk-sensitive graph routing:

*   **Input Layer:** Collects environmental, platform, and mission parameters, including customer target locations, mobile truck and depot coordinates, UAV battery states, payload mass demands, onboard wind measurements, and obstacle spatial maps.
*   **Task Allocation Layer:** Formulates initial operational bounds through three sequential stages:
    1.  *Wind-Aware Clustering:* Groups customer targets into spatial clusters while accounting for ambient wind conditions, battery capacity limits, payload mass, and customer spatial density.
    2.  *Truck Route Planning:* Generates energy-safe ground waypoints and rendezvous locations for the mobile truck platform.
    3.  *UAV-Customer Assignment:* Allocates specific UAVs to customer nodes within defined service regions.
*   **BER Routing Layer (Core):** Executes continuous online trajectory selection using a time-dependent energy graph. This core layer calculates wind-updated edge costs, evaluates continuous return feasibility, selects risk-aware routes, and executes safe abort sequences if return feasibility is violated. Execution feedback and real-time sensing continuously update the time-dependent energy graph throughout mission execution.

### 2.2 Time-Dependent Energy Graph & Edge Cost Formulation

The EWR framework models the low-altitude flight environment as a time-dependent directed energy graph $G_t = (V, E, c_t)$, where $V$ represents flyable waypoints, $E$ denotes directed flight segments, and $c_t(e)$ represents the online edge cost for edge $e \in E$ at decision time $t$.

To balance nominal energy efficiency against environmental risk, the composite edge cost equation is defined as:

$$c_t(e) = E_t(e) + \lambda U_t(e)$$

where $E_t(e)$ is the predicted energy consumption, $U_t(e)$ is a wind-induced uncertainty penalty, and $\lambda \ge 0$ is a user-defined risk-sensitivity coefficient.

#### Explicit Vector Projection & Traversable Energy $E_t(e)$
To account for the directional impact of wind relative to flight orientation, let $\mathbf{u}_\eta$ represent the unit tangent vector along the ground path segment $e$. The delayed online wind estimate $\hat{\mathbf{W}}_t(e) = \hat{\mathbf{W}}(t-\tau_e, e)$ is decomposed into parallel and perpendicular vector components:

$$\hat{W}_\parallel(t,e) = \hat{\mathbf{W}}_t(e)^\top \mathbf{u}_\eta$$

$$\hat{\mathbf{W}}_\perp(t,e) = \hat{\mathbf{W}}_t(e) - \hat{W}_\parallel(t,e)\mathbf{u}_\eta$$

The predicted energy consumption for traversing a segment of length $\ell(e)$ at an estimated path-aligned ground speed $\hat{V}_G(t,e)$ incorporates both longitudinal and lateral power components:

$$E_t(e) = \frac{\ell(e)}{\hat{V}_G(t,e)} \left[ P_0 + P_\parallel(t,e) + P_\perp(t,e) + P_{\text{load}}(e) \right]$$

The multi-rotor platform energy model incorporates physical parameters calibrated against empirical rotary-wing studies (Table I of the paper):
*   $P_0$: Nominal cruise power required in still air (set to $180\text{ W}$ for a platform with nominal airspeed $V_A = 12\text{ m/s}$ and total battery capacity $E_{\text{max}} = 100\text{ Wh}$).
*   $P_\parallel(t,e)$: Propulsion power variation induced by parallel headwind or tailwind components along path direction $\mathbf{u}_\eta$.
*   $P_{\text{load}}(e)$: Payload-induced power consumption, proportional to carried payload mass (up to maximum payload $m_{\text{load}} = 0.5\text{ kg}$).
*   $P_\perp(t,e)$: Additional electrical power required for lateral wind rejection. Because multi-rotor UAVs must tilt and adjust thrust allocation to counteract crosswinds, $P_\perp(t,e)$ models the energy cost of maintaining directional tracking under perpendicular wind forces.

#### Conservative Crosswind Reachability Filter
To prevent a UAV from entering flight segments where crosswinds exceed aerodynamic control limits or push ground speed near zero, EWR enforces a dual reachability filter:

$$\|\hat{\mathbf{W}}_\perp\| + \epsilon_W(t,e) \le \rho_\perp V_A \quad \text{and} \quad \hat{V}_G - \epsilon_W(t,e) > 0$$

where $\hat{\mathbf{W}}_\perp$ is the estimated crosswind vector component, $V_A = 12\text{ m/s}$ is nominal airspeed, $\rho_\perp = 0.75$ represents the lateral control safety margin, and $\epsilon_W(t,e)$ is the upper-bounded estimation error confidence radius.

#### Uncertainty Penalty $U_t(e)$
The penalty function $U_t(e)$ penalizes flight segments characterized by high wind variance, stale data, or rapid environmental shifts:

$$U_t(e) = \beta_W \epsilon_W(t,e) + \beta_\tau \tau_e + \beta_g \|\hat{\mathbf{W}}_t(e) - \hat{\mathbf{W}}_t^-(e)\|$$

where $\tau_e$ represents sensing update delay, $\hat{\mathbf{W}}_t^-(e)$ is the previously accepted wind estimate, and $\beta_W, \beta_\tau, \beta_g$ are non-negative weighting parameters.

### 2.3 Adaptive Safety Margins & Continuous Return Feasibility Checks

To eliminate reliance on static energy reserves, EWR calculates an adaptive safety margin $E_{\text{safe}}(t,e)$ for every candidate edge $e$:

$$E_{\text{safe}}(t,e) = E_{\text{res}} + \alpha_w \sigma_E(t,e) + \alpha_b \Delta E_{\text{soc}}^i(t) + \alpha_p \rho_{\text{phase}}(t)$$

*   $E_{\text{res}}$: Fixed emergency reserve baseline ($10\text{ Wh}$).
*   $\sigma_E(t,e)$: Standard deviation of the wind-aware energy prediction model, scaled by sensitivity parameter $\alpha_w$.
*   $\Delta E_{\text{soc}}^i(t)$: Energy estimation error induced by state-of-charge uncertainty, scaled by $\alpha_b$.
*   $\rho_{\text{phase}}(t)$: Normalized mission-phase risk indicator distinguishing loaded outbound, unloaded return, and final approach phases, scaled by $\alpha_p$.

The maximum wind-induced energy uncertainty along segment $e$ is upper-bounded using a local Lipschitz bound $L_E(e)$:

$$\Delta E_W(t,e) = L_E(e)\epsilon_W(t,e)$$

where $L_E(e)$ represents the local Lipschitz constant bounding the maximum rate of change of the edge energy consumption model with respect to wind estimation perturbations along segment $e$.

#### Continuous Return Feasibility Verification
Before committing a UAV $i$ to a forward segment $e_t$ toward waypoint $u$, EWR verifies whether remaining battery energy $E_i^{\text{rem}}(t)$ satisfies the continuous feasibility inequality:

$$E_i^{\text{rem}}(t) \ge E_t(e_t) + E_t^{\text{ret}}(u) + E_{\text{safe}}(t,e_t) + \Delta E_W(t,e_t)$$

where $E_t^{\text{ret}}(u)$ is the energy required to traverse the return path from waypoint $u$ back to the mobile truck or depot. If a forward edge violates this condition, EWR blocks forward progression and triggers a safe abort sequence, ensuring the UAV retains sufficient energy to return immediately to the mobile platform.

### 2.4 Spatial Routing Dynamics in Evolving Wind Fields

The EWR framework dynamically alters spatial flight trajectories as local wind conditions evolve across time steps $t = 55\text{ s}, 80\text{ s}, 145\text{ s},$ and $190\text{ s}$.

The simulation visualizes the interaction between flight paths and environmental features:
*   **Depot:** Marked by a green circle at the lower-left origin.
*   **Customer Targets:** Represented by black diamond markers spatially distributed across the map.
*   **UAV Positions:** Represented by black triangles labeled NO-1 through NO-4.
*   **Truck Platform Trajectory:** Rendered as a black dashed line showing the ground rendezvous route.

By redirecting UAV trajectories through lower-velocity wind corridors, the system minimizes aerodynamic drag and crosswind compensation power, preserving valid return options back to the moving truck throughout the mission.

## 3. Quantitative Performance & Preventative Safety Analysis

### 3.1 Success Rate and Return Failure Comparisons

The authors evaluated EWR against six baseline routing strategies across synthetic delivery graphs using replayed wind logs from a public ATS truck-UAV delivery dataset. Evaluation settings included a full battery budget ($B = 100$, corresponding to full $100\text{ Wh}$ capacity) and a restricted low-battery budget ($B = 50$, corresponding to $50\text{ Wh}$).

The primary evaluation metrics include:
1.  **Mission Success Rate ($R_{\text{succ}}$):** The percentage of test missions where all assigned customer deliveries were completed and all UAVs safely returned to the depot or mobile truck.
2.  **Return Failure Rate ($R_{\text{fail}}$):** The percentage of missions where a UAV suffered complete inflight energy depletion due to dynamic wind disturbances.

*Note: $R_{\text{succ}}$ and $R_{\text{fail}}$ do not sum to 100%; the remaining outcomes correspond to safe aborts initiated by EWR when return feasibility thresholds were breached.*

As shown in Figure 4(a) of the paper, EWR outperforms all baseline strategies across both battery settings while suppressing return failure rates:

| Routing Method | Success Rate $R_{\text{succ}}$ (%) [$B=100$] | Success Rate $R_{\text{succ}}$ (%) [$B=50$] | Return Failure $R_{\text{fail}}$ (%) [$B=100$] | Return Failure $R_{\text{fail}}$ (%) [$B=50$] |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **SP-NoWind** | ~61% | ~36% | ~16.5% | ~21.5% |
| **Energy-SP** | ~67% | ~43% | ~13.8% | ~18.5% |
| **Initial-Wind-SP** | ~73% | ~50% | ~9.8% | ~14.3% |
| **Online-Replan** | ~79% | ~58% | ~7.0% | ~10.7% |
| **D* Lite** | ~81% | ~60% | ~5.8% | ~9.5% |
| **Greedy-Energy** | ~68% | ~45% | ~13.0% | ~17.8% |
| **EWR (Proposed)** | **~92%** | **>75%** | **<1.0%** | **~1.8%** |

Static baselines (SP-NoWind, Energy-SP) suffer severe degradation under low energy budgets ($B=50$), failing in over 18–21% of test flights due to unmodeled wind resistance. Reactive replanners (Online-Replan, D* Lite) adapt to updated wind fields but still yield 5.8–10.7% failure rates because they lack explicit return feasibility bounds. In contrast, EWR maintains a mission success rate of ~92% under $B=100$ and >75% under $B=50$, while keeping return failure rates below 2% across all trials.

### 3.2 Energy Safety Buffer & Residual Margin Metrics

To quantify energy safety throughout mission execution, Li et al. defined the minimum residual energy margin metric $M_{\text{min}}$:

$$M_{\text{min}} = \min_t \left[ E_i^{\text{rem}}(t) - E_t(e_t) - E_t^{\text{ret}}(u_t) - E_{\text{safe}}(t,e_t) - \Delta E_W(t,e_t) \right]$$

A positive $M_{\text{min}}$ confirms that a UAV maintained a valid energy buffer during every flight decision step. A negative $M_{\text{min}}$ indicates that the platform entered an energy-unsafe regime where safe return could no longer be guaranteed.

As shown in Figure 4(b) of the paper:
*   **Baseline Behaviors:** Static and energy-oblivious baselines yield **negative average minimum margins** (dropping to $-5.0\text{ Wh}$ to $-7.5\text{ Wh}$ under $B=50$). Reactive replanners (Online-Replan, D* Lite) hover near zero ($1.5\text{ Wh}$ to $2.5\text{ Wh}$), leaving minimal buffer against dynamic gusts.
*   **EWR Behavior:** EWR maintains a **strictly positive residual margin** across both energy settings ($6.91\text{ Wh}$ under $B=100$), confirming that the adaptive safety margin $E_{\text{safe}}$ prevents the UAV from operating near depletion thresholds.

### 3.3 Real-World Wind Log Validation (ASOS Data)

To evaluate performance under unmodeled real-world atmospheric conditions, the authors replayed surface weather logs from an Automated Surface Observing System (ASOS) weather station, downloaded via the Iowa Environmental Mesonet. These logs contain continuous time-series data including abrupt wind direction changes and high-speed gust surges.

The quantitative results under real-world ASOS wind log replays ($B = 100$) are detailed below:

| Method | Success Rate $R_{\text{succ}}$ (%) | Return Failure Rate $R_{\text{fail}}$ (%) | Min Energy Margin $M_{\text{min}}$ (Wh) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **SP-NoWind** | $58.7 \pm 3.9$ | $19.6 \pm 2.8$ | $-6.42 \pm 1.31$ |
| **Energy-SP** | $64.3 \pm 3.6$ | $16.8 \pm 2.5$ | $-4.75 \pm 1.18$ |
| **Online-Replan** | $76.9 \pm 3.1$ | $8.7 \pm 1.9$ | $1.62 \pm 0.74$ |
| **D* Lite** | $78.4 \pm 2.9$ | $7.9 \pm 1.7$ | $2.08 \pm 0.69$ |
| **EWR (Proposed)** | **$86.5 \pm 2.4$** | **$2.8 \pm 0.9$** | **$6.91 \pm 0.86$** |

EWR's performance advantage is most pronounced during sudden wind shifts and strong gust events. Under these non-stationary conditions, baseline planners without uncertainty margins underestimate return power demands, resulting in return failure rates of $19.6\%$ for SP-NoWind and $8.7\%$ for Online-Replan. EWR suppresses return failures to $2.8\%$ while maintaining an average minimum safety margin of $6.91\text{ Wh}$.

## 4. Key Takeaways for Failure-First AI Safety & Open Limitations

### Core Lessons for Embodied AI Safety

*   **Risk-Sensitive Margins vs. Nominal Optimality:** Optimizing flight paths based on nominal energy models or dynamic shortest-path heuristics creates structural safety vulnerabilities under environmental disturbances. Incorporating explicit uncertainty bounds ($\epsilon_W$) into graph edge costs prevents catastrophic return-feasibility failures far more effectively than re-optimizing nominal path length on the fly.
*   **Continuous Return Feasibility Verification:** Reactive replanning algorithms (e.g., D* Lite or online shortest path) are insufficient if they uncritically trust noisy, delayed state observations. Safe mission execution under dynamic disturbances requires enforcing continuous return-to-home feasibility constraints before committing to forward actions.

### Acknowledged Limitations

Li et al. identify two primary limitations in the current EWR formulation:

1.  **Absence of Predictive Wind-Gust Modeling:** The framework relies on delayed sensor estimates and bounded uncertainty margins ($\epsilon_W$), but lacks forward predictive modeling for sudden, unmodeled, or extreme localized wind gusts.
2.  **Exclusion of Battery Aging Effects:** The energy consumption model assumes nominal battery capacity and constant discharge dynamics, omitting capacity degradation, internal resistance growth, and thermal stress associated with real-world battery aging.
