### 1. Introduction: The Limits of VQA-Formulated Vision-Language-Action Driving

End-to-end autonomous driving (E2E-AD) architectures have increasingly evolved from traditional modular pipelines—which separate perception, motion forecasting, and path planning into sequential subsystems—toward integrated Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. Modular stacks are prone to error propagation across boundaries, where sub-optimal upstream detections compound into hazardous downstream motion commands. In contrast, E2E-AD frameworks map raw visual and sensory inputs directly to motion planning objectives. Incorporating Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and VLAs allows autonomous agents to leverage natural-language instruction following and open-vocabulary reasoning for visuomotor control.

However, as demonstrated by Sun et al. (2026), a core architectural conflict restricts current VLA-based autonomous driving systems: most mainstream models formulate driving as a Visual Question Answering (VQA) task. Under this paradigm, driving maneuvers are generated as textual token sequences in response to prompt queries. Sun et al. identify three primary failure modes inherent to standard VQA-formulated VLAs:

1. **Weak 3D Geometric and Spatial Reasoning:** Most VLM backbones rely on 2D image pretraining. When processing 3D spatial coordinates as digit-by-digit text tokens, these models lack spatial grounding, resulting in inaccurate distance estimation, poor 3D topological awareness, and persistent waypoint drift.
2. **Fragile Multimodal Sensor Alignment:** Heterogeneous sensor modalities—such as camera visual streams, LiDAR point clouds, and text navigation commands—exhibit structural feature misalignment. Conventional early or late fusion strategies lack formal mathematical bounds, leading to perception breakdown and semantic misalignment in complex, long-tail driving scenarios.
3. **Single-Point Planning Collapse and Lack of Interpretability:** Standard VQA-style VLAs typically output a single deterministic trajectory or rely on uninterpretable black-box decision-making. In safety-critical edge cases, this design leads to mode collapse or unrecoverable action failure. While post-hoc reinforcement learning techniques such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) attempt to enforce safety through reward-driven parameter updates, they remain computationally expensive, implicitly constrained, and uninterpretable.

To address these vulnerabilities, Sun et al. (2026) introduced a framework in their paper, *"A Collaborative Multi-Modality Interaction for VLA-based End-to-End Autonomous Driving."* Their approach combines mathematically constrained multimodal sensor fusion with explicit trajectory candidate optimization, achieving reliable real-time motion planning without requiring policy-parameter updates like GRPO.

#### Architectural Comparison of E2E Driving Pipelines

*   **Traditional E2E Pipelines:** Rely on sequential modular perception (e.g., bounding box detection feeding into motion planning) or flattened sensory-to-control neural mappings. They preserve 3D spatial structures poorly, lack natural-language instruction control, and offer limited fallback mechanisms when sensors fall out of calibration.
*   **Standard VQA-Style VLAs:** Treat driving as an autoregressive text generation task, predicting waypoints as text digits from 2D visual inputs. They suffer from mode collapse, poor 3D spatial reasoning, and implicit decision-making, often relying on policy update schemes (e.g., GRPO) to enforce safety rules.
*   **Proposed System Architecture (Sun et al., 2026):** Combines Affinity-Guided Optimal Transport (AGOT) and Distribution-Consistent Modality Transfer (DCMT) for robust cross-sensor alignment. It pairs Discrete Flow Matching (DFM) candidate trajectory generation with online Perception-Oriented Trajectory Refinement (POTR) using explicit risk cost maps, ensuring interpretable control without fine-tuning underlying policy weights.

### 2. Heterogeneous Sensor Fusion: Mechanics of AGOT and DCMT

To resolve cross-modal misalignment across heterogeneous sensors (camera RGB images, LiDAR point clouds, and language instructions), Sun et al. design two distinct perception alignment modules: **Affinity-Guided Optimal Transport (AGOT)** and **Distribution-Consistent Modality Transfer (DCMT)**.

#### Affinity-Guided Optimal Transport (AGOT)
AGOT establishes structured, two-way interaction between main and auxiliary sensor modalities (e.g., treating LiDAR BEV tokens $B = \{b_j\}_{j=1}^{N_b}$ as the main modality and Camera visual tokens $V = \{v_i\}_{i=1}^{N_v}$ as the auxiliary modality).

First, a learnable affinity matrix $S_{BV}$ is computed via cross-attention and a multi-layer perceptron:
$$S_{BV} = \text{MLP}(\text{CrossAttn}(V, B))$$

The non-negative transportation cost matrix $C_{BV}$ is derived via a mapping $C_{BV} = \phi(-S_{BV})$. The linear kernel between tokens is defined as $\kappa(v_i, b_j) = v_i^T W_{d \times d} b_j$, where $W_{d \times d}$ must be a positive semi-definite matrix to maintain valid metric space properties. Applying Theorem 1 (Orthogonal Diagonalization), any real symmetric matrix $W$ can be decomposed as $W = U^T \Lambda U$, where $U$ is an orthogonal matrix and $\Lambda = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_d)$ contains the eigenvalues. Thus, the kernel is expressed as:
$$\kappa(v_i, b_j) = (U v_i)^T \Lambda (U b_j)$$

Matrix $W_{d \times d}$ is positive semi-definite if and only if it is symmetric ($W^T = W$) and all its eigenvalues are non-negative ($\lambda_i \ge 0$). Sun et al. formulate an **affinity kernel regularization loss** ($L_{reg}$) to explicitly enforce these mathematical constraints during training:
$$L_{reg} = \sum_{W \in S_{BV}} \left( |W^T - W| + |\lambda_i - |\lambda_i|| \right)$$

Next, AGOT formulates feature alignment as a discrete optimal transport problem under entropy regularization. Using the Sinkhorn algorithm, it solves for the optimal transport plan matrix $P^*$, yielding an **optimal transport regularization loss** ($L_{ot}$):
$$L_{ot} = ||P^* \cdot \tilde{1} - \mu||_2^2 + ||(P^*)^T \cdot \tilde{1} - \nu||_2^2 + \sum_{i,j} P^*_{i,j} ||v_i - b_j||_2^2 - \sum_{i,j} P^*_{i,j} \log P^*_{i,j}$$

The resulting transport plan aggregates cross-modal features while preserving modality-specific structures: $\tilde{V} = V \oplus P^* \cdot B$ and $\tilde{B} = B \oplus (P^*)^T \cdot V$.

#### Distribution-Consistent Modality Transfer (DCMT)
While AGOT aligns geometric tokens, high-level natural language navigation prompts ($T = \{t_i\}_{i=1}^{N_t}$) reside on fundamentally different feature manifolds than low-level camera and LiDAR observations. DCMT bridges this domain gap by projecting all modality tokens into a unified Gaussian latent space using a shared, invertible normalizing flow $F_\theta$:
$$z_i^m = F_\theta(m_i) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_m, \Sigma_m), \quad m \in \{t, c, b\}$$

The normalizing flow is trained by maximizing the conditional log-likelihood via the **unified flow loss** ($L_{unf}$):
$$L_{unf} = -\sum_{m \in \{t,c,b\}} \sum_i \left( \log p(z_i^m) + \log |\det J_F| \right)$$

where $J_F$ is the Jacobian matrix of the transformation $F_\theta$. The absolute determinant $|\det J_F|$ accounts for local volume changes under the invertible mapping, preserving probability measure integrity when transforming tokens into the Gaussian manifold. To minimize distribution discrepancies across modalities, Sun et al. enforce a **cross-modal distribution consistency loss** ($L_{consis}$) that minimizes the pairwise Sinkhorn distance ($D_e$) across latent representations:
$$L_{consis} = D_e(P_t, P_c) + D_e(P_t, P_b) + D_e(P_b, P_c)$$

| Module Feature | Affinity-Guided Optimal Transport (AGOT) | Distribution-Consistent Modality Transfer (DCMT) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Core Purpose** | Establishes two-way semantic correspondence and feature aggregation between main and auxiliary physical sensor tokens. | Projects heterogeneous high-level (text) and low-level (vision/LiDAR) distributions into a common latent space. |
| **Mathematical / Loss Components** | Learnable Affinity Matrix ($S_{BV}$), Affinity Kernel Regularization ($L_{reg}$), Entropy-Regularized Sinkhorn Transport ($L_{ot}$). | Shared Invertible Normalizing Flow ($F_\theta$), Likelihood Maximization Loss ($L_{unf}$), Sinkhorn Distribution Consistency Loss ($L_{consis}$). |
| **Target Modalities** | Heterogeneous spatial sensors (Camera Visual Tokens $\leftrightarrow$ LiDAR BEV Tokens). | All system tokens (Camera, LiDAR BEV, and Natural-Language Navigation Prompts). |

### 3. Multi-Trajectory Generation and Perception-Oriented Trajectory Refinement (POTR)

To prevent action brittleness and single-point plan collapse, Sun et al. decouple raw action output into a two-phase planning pipeline: candidate trajectory generation followed by online perception-oriented refinement.

#### Candidate Generation via DFM
Sun et al. formulate motion planning as a conditional multi-trajectory generation task built on Discrete Flow Matching (DFM). Unlike baseline methods such as VADv2 that rely on predefined anchor trajectory dictionaries or auxiliary classification heads—which discretize the action space and risk missing un-anchored continuous maneuvers—DFM generates paths via continuous flow matching. By saving intermediate flow states across $K$ denoising steps, the model detokenizes these states into a set of diverse candidate trajectories $\mathcal{T} = \{\tilde{\tau}_{(1)}, \tilde{\tau}_{(2)}, \dots, \tilde{\tau}_{(K)}\}$, where each candidate comprises an 8-waypoint sequence $\tilde{\tau} = \{(x_t, y_t)\}_{t=1}^8$. This mechanism preserves continuous intention diversity directly along the denoising timeline without adding extra parameters.

The flow matching module is optimized using a composite planning loss $L_{plan}$:
$$L_{plan} = \min_k d(\tilde{\tau}_{(k)}, \tau_{gt}) - \sum_{i \neq j} ||\tilde{\tau}_{(i)} - \tilde{\tau}_{(j)}||_2^2 + \sum_{k=1}^{K-1} \max(0, d_{k+1} - d_k)$$

This objective balances three distinct properties:
1. **Matching Loss ($L_{match}$):** Forces at least one candidate path to match ground-truth expert motion.
2. **Diversity Loss ($L_{diversity}$):** Penalizes trajectory convergence across intermediate steps to prevent mode collapse.
3. **Consistency Loss ($L_{consist}$):** Enforces a coarse-to-fine trajectory error reduction along the denoising timeline.

#### Driving Risk Cost Map Construction
Rather than updating policy parameters online via reinforcement learning, Sun et al. construct an explicit spatial driving risk cost map $\Gamma(\tilde{\tau})$ using real-time 3D bounding box detections $B_j = \{c_x, c_y, l, w, yaw\}$ and BEV map segmentations $S \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times w \times c}$. The trajectory optimization objective contains three explicit terms:
$$\Gamma(\tilde{\tau}) = \Gamma_{prior} + \Gamma_{risk} + \Gamma_{smooth}$$

*   **Prior Cost ($\Gamma_{prior}$):** Constrains candidate path adjustments to remain near the high-probability driving manifold produced by the flow model:
    $$\Gamma_{prior} = \sum_i (\tau_i - \tilde{\tau}_i)^T (\tau_i - \tilde{\tau}_i)$$
*   **Driving Risk Cost ($\Gamma_{risk}$):** Combines a Detection Risk Cost Map (DRCM) and a Semantic Risk Cost Map (SRCM):
    $$\Gamma_{risk} = \sum_c P(c | x_i, y_i) \omega_c + \sum_j \sigma_j \phi(D_j(x_i, y_i))$$
    where $\phi(D_j(x_i, y_i)) = \exp(-D_j(x_i, y_i) / \sigma)$ represents the spatial distance decay field around obstacle $j$, governed by the scalar decay parameter $\sigma$. The multiplier $\sigma_j$ denotes the obstacle risk weight for bounding box $j$, while $P(c | x_i, y_i)$ maps structural map costs (e.g., drivable areas, sidewalks, stop lines) multiplied by category risk weights $\omega_c$.
*   **Smoothness Cost ($\Gamma_{smooth}$):** Penalizes path curvature ($\kappa_i$) and discrete second-order waypoint variations to prevent abrupt control inputs:
    $$\Gamma_{smooth} = \sum_i ||\kappa_i||_2^2 + \sum_i ||\tau_{i+1} - 2\tau_i + \tau_{i-1}||_2^2$$

#### Online Trajectory Optimization via LM
The Perception-Oriented Trajectory Refinement (POTR) mechanism optimizes each 16-dimensional continuous waypoint vector $\tilde{\tau} = [x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2, \dots, x_8, y_8]^T$ online using the non-linear Levenberg-Marquardt (LM) algorithm. Because LM acts directly on spatial waypoint coordinates rather than fine-tuning neural network weights, it enforces deterministic safety constraints without altering the frozen VLA policy parameters.

The algorithmic execution pipeline proceeds through six steps:

1. **Token Integration:** Concatenate multimodal representations $Z = [\hat{V}, \hat{B}, T, E]$, where $E$ represents ego-vehicle status tokens.
2. **DFM Denoising & Sampling:** Execute $K$ denoising steps to output candidate trajectory paths $\mathcal{T} = \{\tilde{\tau}_{(1)}, \tilde{\tau}_{(2)}, \dots, \tilde{\tau}_{(K)}\}$.
3. **Cost Map Construction:** Build local DRCM dynamic obstacle fields and SRCM road segment probability maps from parallel perception heads.
4. **Online Path Optimization:** Solve for the optimal continuous spatial path for each individual candidate trajectory $\tilde{\tau}_{(k)}$ using the non-linear Levenberg-Marquardt (LM) algorithm:
   $$\tilde{\tau}_{(k)}^* = \arg\min_{\tau} \Gamma(\tilde{\tau}_{(k)})$$
5. **Safety Scoring:** Recalculate total risk cost $\Gamma(\tilde{\tau}_{(k)}^*)$ for all optimized paths to assign a numerical trajectory score:
   $$score_k = \exp(-\Gamma(\tilde{\tau}_{(k)}^*))$$
6. **Final Selection:** Select the highest-scoring candidate as the final actuation trajectory:
   $$\tilde{\tau}^*_{best} = \arg\max_k score_k$$

### 4. Benchmark Performance & Safety Evaluation

Sun et al. evaluated their framework across open-loop motion prediction, closed-loop CARLA simulation, and multi-modal 3D perception benchmarks.

#### Motion Prediction and Closed-Loop Performance

On the **NAVSIM** open-loop planning benchmark, the proposed system achieved a Predictive Driver Model Score (PDMS) of **92.2** and an Extended PDM Score (EPDMS) of **87.0**, outperforming both traditional end-to-end baselines (GoalFlow, TransFuser) and contemporary VLA architectures (WAM-Flow, DrivingGPT, AutoVLA). Sub-metric evaluations confirmed zero-collision compliance (NC of 99.0), high driving area compliance (DAC of 98.5), and strong ego progress (EP of 88.6).

In closed-loop testing on **Bench2Drive** (50 interactive CARLA simulation scenarios), the framework recorded a **77.45% Driving Score (DS)** and a **56.00% Success Rate (SR)**, reducing pedestrian infractions (CP) to **0.55** and vehicle collisions (CV) to **2.82**. Long-horizon evaluations on **nuScenes** demonstrated lower trajectory displacement error (L2 Avg of **0.30m**) and reduced collision rates (**0.21%** overall average) across 1s, 2s, and 3s horizons compared to VLM planners such as SpaceDrive and OmniDrive.

#### Ablation Insights
Ablation studies reported by the authors quantify the contributions of specific module components:
*   **Cumulative Planning Loss Build-Up:** As reported in Table 9, training with matching loss alone ($L_{match}$) yields a NAVSIM PDMS of **86.9**. Adding the diversity loss ($L_{match} + L_{diversity}$) increases PDMS to **87.5**, demonstrating that trajectory diversity prevents local mode collapse. Incorporating the consistency loss ($L_{match} + L_{diversity} + L_{consist}$) achieves the maximum PDMS of **92.2** while increasing Ego Progress (EP) from 85.0 to 88.6.
*   **Cost Map Components:** Disabling either the Detection Risk Cost Map (DRCM) or the Semantic Risk Cost Map (SRCM) caused PDMS to drop from 92.2 down to 85.1 and 85.6, respectively, confirming the necessity of perception-derived risk fields during online refinement.
*   **Optimal Transport & Normalizing Flow Losses:** Excluding the AGOT regularization loss ($L_{reg}$) or OT loss ($L_{ot}$) lowered nuScenes 3D detection mAP by 2.0% and 2.7%. Omitting the DCMT consistency loss ($L_{consis}$) reduced planning PDMS by 0.4.

### 5. Architectural Limitations and Strategic Takeaways for AI Safety Practitioners

#### Acknowledged Technical Limitations
*   **Optimal Transport Computational Overhead:** Computing Sinkhorn transport plans ($P^*$) across dense multimodal token matrices introduces non-trivial latency and memory overhead. Sparse or hierarchical transport mechanisms are needed to optimize real-time performance.
*   **Perception Error Cascading in POTR:** Online trajectory refinement relies directly on real-time 3D detection bounding boxes and BEV segmentation maps. Significant false negatives or segmentation artifacts in upstream perception heads can distort the risk cost map ($\Gamma_{risk}$), potentially degrading path optimization quality.

#### Key Strategic Takeaways for AI Safety Practitioners

1. **Structural Post-Processing Outperforms Implicit Policy Optimization:** Rather than relying on black-box reinforcement learning updates (e.g., GRPO) to fine-tune VLA control behavior, explicit online trajectory refinement via Perception-Oriented Trajectory Refinement (POTR) enforces verifiable safety constraints through interpretable cost functions (prior, risk, and smoothness).
2. **Mathematically Bound Sensor Fusion for Edge-Case Perception:** Standard attention-based sensor concatenation often fails during long-tail sensor degradation. Structuring multimodal interactions via optimal transport (AGOT) and normalizing flow projection into Gaussian latent spaces (DCMT) provides mathematically constrained feature alignment, mitigating cross-sensor semantic drift.
3. **Multi-Candidate Planning Prevents Single-Point Action Collapse:** Formulating VLA action generation as multi-trajectory candidate sampling (via Discrete Flow Matching) paired with online cost-map scoring avoids the mode collapse inherent in standard VQA-formulated visual question-answering pipelines.
