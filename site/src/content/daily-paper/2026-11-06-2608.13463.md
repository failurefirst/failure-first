### 1. Introduction: The Cross-Domain Generalization Wall

Modern image classification models achieve exceptional performance when trained and evaluated on isolated, task-specific datasets. However, real-world vision systems—such as those embedded in autonomous robotics, medical diagnostics, and mobile AI assistants—must operate across diverse visual environments. When exposed to broad cross-dataset distribution shifts (e.g., transitioning between natural photographs, diagnostic medical scans, subtle human facial expressions, and high-altitude satellite imagery), single-architecture classifiers frequently suffer severe performance degradation.

In benchmark evaluations conducted by Perkins et al., this fragility highlights fundamental trade-offs across conventional deep learning paradigms:
* **Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs):** Exemplified by architectures like ResNet-50, CNNs excel at capturing localized textures, fine edges, and spatial hierarchies. However, their fixed inductive biases make them sensitive to distribution shifts, frequently resulting in cross-domain overfitting when forced to evaluate out-of-distribution visual inputs.
* **Self-Supervised Learning (SSL):** Attention-based representation learners, such as DINOv2 and DINOv3, extract structural visual features without relying on dense human annotations. While SSL backbones demonstrate broad transferability, fine-tuning a single backbone on a specialized downstream task often restricts the model's feature space, degrading performance across disjoint visual domains.
* **Vision-Language Models (VLMs):** Contrastive architectures like CLIP map images and natural language into a shared embedding space, enabling broad zero-shot alignment and scene-level semantic understanding. However, VLMs often exhibit reduced accuracy when evaluating fine-grained, highly specialized anatomical or domain-specific textures.

To mitigate single-backbone vulnerabilities, machine learning engineers traditionally deploy ensemble architectures. **Dense fusion ensembles** concatenate representations or average output probabilities across multiple backbones, but they incur high computational overhead by executing every constituent model for every input frame. **Learned dynamic routing frameworks**—such as Expert Gate or Neural Logit Controllers (NLC)—route inputs to specific models to reduce inference latency. However, traditional routers suffer from high structural rigidity: introducing a new task or domain requires re-architecting and retraining the entire meta-classifier. Furthermore, learned neural routers operate as opaque black boxes, offering safety researchers and auditors no semantic visibility into why a specific backbone was selected for a given input.

To address these operational and safety limitations, Perkins et al. introduced **ARMDIL** (**A**daptive **R**outer for **M**ulti-**D**omain **I**mage Classification with **L**LMs). ARMDIL replaces rigid, black-box meta-classifiers with a zero-shot, prompt-driven Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM) agent. By combining high-level visual reasoning with dynamic expert selection, ARMDIL provides an interpretable and adaptable alternative to traditional trained neural ensembles.

### 2. Deconstructing the ARMDIL Architecture

The ARMDIL framework routes incoming images to heterogeneous domain experts through a multi-stage operational pipeline orchestrated by an MLLM agent.

```
                  [Input Image]
                        │
        ┌───────────────┴───────────────┐
        ▼                               ▼
 [Image Preprocessing]     [Auxiliary Quality Metrics]
 (Resize & Normalize)      • Perceptual Blur Score
                           • Mean Brightness
                           • Grayscale Contrast
                           • Wavelet Noise MAD
        │                               │
        └───────────────┬───────────────┘
                        ▼
         [Gemma-4-12B MLLM Router Agent]
       (Visual Semantics + CoT Reasoning)
                        │
  ┌─────────────┬───────┴───────┬─────────────┬─────────────┐
  ▼             ▼               ▼             ▼             ▼
`GENERAL`    `FACIAL`      `GEOGRAPHIC`   `MEDICAL`     `UNSURE`
  │             │               │             │             │
(DINOv2)     (DINOv3)        (CLIP)      (ResNet-50) (Balanced DINOv3)
  │             │               │             │             │
  └─────────────┴───────┬───────┴─────────────┴─────────────┘
                        ▼
          [Unified N=38 Head Output]
```

#### Operational Pipeline

1. **Preprocessing & Quality Signal Generation:** The input image is resized and normalized. Simultaneously, an automated image-quality assessment pipeline calculates four explicit heuristic metrics derived from established signal-processing algorithms:
   * **Blur score:** Evaluated using the no-reference perceptual blur metric introduced by Crété-Roffet et al. (2007), rating perceptual blur on a continuous scale from `0.0` (sharp) to `1.0` (blurry).
   * **Mean brightness:** Calculated as the mean intensity of image pixels following grayscale conversion (`0.0` = pure black, `1.0` = pure white).
   * **Contrast:** Derived from the standard deviation of grayscale pixel intensities (`0.0` = extremely low contrast, `0.25+` = very high contrast).
   * **Noise estimate:** Computed using Donoho & Johnstone's (1994) median absolute deviation (MAD) of wavelet detail coefficients (`0.0` = noise-free, `0.10+` = highly noisy).
2. **Unified Label Space Construction:** All constituent expert models are fine-tuned with a shared, unified $N=38$ classification head that aggregates class labels across four diverse public datasets:
   $$\text{CIFAR10 } (10 \text{ classes}) + \text{FER2013 } (7 \text{ classes}) + \text{EuroSAT } (10 \text{ classes}) + \text{OrganAMNIST } (11 \text{ classes}) = 38 \text{ total logits}$$
   Structuring all linear heads over this joint, disjoint label space ensures that even if an image is routed to a sub-optimal expert, the selected model outputs a valid, mathematically well-formed logit distribution across the entire benchmark label space.
3. **Agent Decision Engine Prompting:** The raw visual input and its auxiliary quality statistics are provided to a quantized local MLLM router (Gemma-4-12B UD-Q5). Using Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning, the MLLM evaluates visual concepts alongside heuristic statistics to categorize the image into one of five domain aliases: `GENERAL`, `FACIAL`, `GEOGRAPHIC`, `MEDICAL`, or `UNSURE`.
4. **Dynamic Expert Execution & Scapegoat Routing:** The MLLM delegates classification to the corresponding domain expert backbone. If the MLLM router exhibits ambiguity, it outputs the `UNSURE` alias, which redirects the sample to the generalist baseline model possessing the highest overall validation accuracy: **Balanced DINOv3 (ViT-L/16)**.

#### Multi-Domain Dataset Mapping

Perkins et al. structured their multi-domain benchmark using four public image datasets, mapping each dataset to an abstract domain alias within the MLLM system prompt:

| Benchmark Dataset & Assigned Alias | Visual Description & Target Scope |
| :--- | :--- |
| **CIFAR10**<br>`GENERAL` | Natural, object-centric images encompassing animals, vehicles, and everyday items across 10 balanced categories. |
| **FER2013**<br>`FACIAL` | Close-up human faces, portraits, and expression crops categorized across seven distinct emotional states. |
| **EuroSAT**<br>`GEOGRAPHIC` | Multi-spectral high-altitude satellite imagery, land-cover perspectives, and aerial terrain environments across 10 classes. |
| **OrganAMNIST** (O-MNIST)<br>`MEDICAL` | Diagnostic 2D medical scans, pathology cross-sections, and anatomical CT slices covering 11 human organ categories. |

### 3. Architecture Vulnerabilities & Expert Specialization

To select domain experts for ARMDIL, Perkins et al. evaluated candidate backbones across three architectural families trained under domain-skewed (70% target domain bias) and balanced distributions. Their empirical findings highlight distinct capability boundaries and failure modes for each backbone type.

```
       [Architectural Capability & Specialization Profile]

       ResNet-50 (CNN) ──────────► Fine Texture & Local Edges
                                   (Peak: Medical Scans / OrganAMNIST)

       DINOv2/v3 (SSL) ──────────► Structural & Feature Stability
                                   (Peak: Facial Expressions & Objects)

       CLIP ViT-L/14* (VLM) ─────► Scene-Level Semantic Alignment
                                   (Peak: Satellite Terrain / EuroSAT)

       *Note: Text describes ViT-L/14 while paper tables list ViT-L/16.
```

* **ResNet-50 (Convolutional Neural Networks):** In the benchmark evaluations conducted by Perkins et al., ResNet-50 demonstrated high parameter efficiency and strong performance when extracting localized features, fine anatomical edges, and high-frequency visual textures. As a result, ResNet-50 achieved the top classification performance on diagnostic medical scans (97.20% top-1 accuracy on OrganAMNIST). However, ResNet-50 exhibited marked sensitivity to distribution shifts and a tendency toward cross-domain overfitting when trained on multi-domain data.
* **DINOv2 & DINOv3 (Self-Supervised Learning):** Attention-based representation learners displayed superior structural stability and broad representation capacity. DINOv3 (ViT-L/16) achieved state-of-the-art results on nuanced facial emotion recognition (70.49% top-1 accuracy on FER2013), outperforming all competing backbones. DINOv2 (ViT-L/14) achieved top performance on natural object classification (99.24% top-1 accuracy on CIFAR10).
* **CLIP ViT-L/14 + LoRA (Vision-Language Models):** Parameter-efficient fine-tuning via LoRA adapters inserted into OpenCLIP ViT-L/14 enabled strong scene-level semantic alignment. CLIP outperformed all candidate architectures on high-altitude aerial terrain (98.56% top-1 accuracy on EuroSAT), benefiting from broad conceptual representations rather than localized texture extraction. *(Note: Perkins et al. report using OpenCLIP ViT-L/14 in Section 3.2.3 of their paper, while labeling the model as CLIP ViT-L/16 in Table 2; both designations refer to the same VLM expert within their experimental setup).*

#### Backbone Capability & Vulnerability Comparison

The empirical findings reported in Tables 2 and 3 of the paper are summarized below:

| Architectural Family | Core Strengths | Primary Vulnerabilities | Peak Target Domain Performance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **ResNet-50 (CNN)** | High parameter efficiency; precise local edge and high-frequency texture extraction. | Sensitive to distribution shift; prone to cross-domain feature overfitting. | `MEDICAL` (OrganAMNIST: **97.20% Acc** / **96.97% F1**) |
| **DINOv2 / DINOv3 (SSL)** | Attention-driven structural representations; high resilience under shift. | Greater memory foot-print; higher computational cost during downstream adaptation. | `FACIAL` (FER2013: **70.49% Acc** / **67.75% F1**) & `GENERAL` (CIFAR10: **99.24% Acc** / **99.28% F1**) |
| **CLIP ViT-L/14* (VLM)** | Global scene-level semantic alignment; strong domain resilience. | Lower accuracy on fine-grained, localized anatomical textures. | `GEOGRAPHIC` (EuroSAT: **98.56% Acc** / **98.51% F1**) |

### 4. Empirical Evaluation: ARMDIL vs. Traditional Routers & Baselines

Perkins et al. benchmarked ARMDIL against individual backbones, an unweighted Majority Vote (MV) ensemble, a fully trained ResNet-18 Neural Network Router (NN-Router), and an ideal theoretical "Oracle" router (which assumes 100% domain routing accuracy).

#### Benchmark Performance Comparison

Performance metrics across individual datasets and the global unified dataset are detailed below:

| Method / Router Paradigm | CIFAR10 Acc / F1 (%) | EuroSAT Acc / F1 (%) | FER2013 Acc / F1 (%) | OrganAMNIST Acc / F1 (%) | Overall Acc / Macro F1 (%) |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **DINOv3 (Balanced Backbone)** | 99.05 / 99.06 | 97.24 / 97.19 | 69.82 / 66.63 | 92.52 / 92.22 | 89.61 / 89.52 |
| **Majority Vote Ensemble (MV)** | 98.87 / 98.87 | 98.42 / 98.38 | 68.35 / 64.64 | 96.25 / 95.84 | 90.47 / 89.43 |
| **NN-Router (ResNet-18 Meta-Classifier)** | **99.13** / **99.18** | **98.56** / **98.52** | 69.81 / 66.98 | **97.22** / **96.95** | **91.18** / 90.41 |
| **ARMDIL (Gemma-4-12B MLLM Router)** | 99.02 / 99.05 | 97.91 / 97.88 | **70.49** / **67.73** | 96.69 / 96.38 | 90.78 / **90.71** |
| *Theoretical Oracle Router* | *99.24 / 99.28* | *98.56 / 98.51* | *70.49 / 67.75* | *97.20 / 96.97* | *91.04 / 90.99* |

#### Key Analytical Insights

* **Overall Accuracy & F1 Performance:** ARMDIL achieved **90.78% top-1 overall accuracy** and a **90.71% global macro F1 score** across the unified test dataset. This performance surpasses the best individual non-routed backbone (balanced DINOv3 at 89.61%) by 1.17% in accuracy and outperforms the Majority Vote baseline by 0.31% in accuracy and 1.28% in macro F1.
* **Analytical Nuance in Macro F1 vs. Top-1 Accuracy:** While the trained 4-class ResNet-18 NN-Router achieved slightly higher overall top-1 accuracy (91.18% vs. 90.78%), ARMDIL outperformed the NN-Router on overall macro F1 score (**90.71% vs. 90.41%**). This occurs because macro F1 treats all four evaluation domains equally. ARMDIL achieved near-perfect routing accuracy (**99.82% true positive rate**) on FER2013—the most difficult and class-skewed domain—where it yielded a dataset F1 score of **67.73%** (compared to **66.98%** for the NN-Router). Because global macro F1 averages per-dataset F1 scores equally across all four domains, ARMDIL's superior accuracy on the hardest dataset elevated its overall macro F1 score above the NN-Router, despite minor routing hesitations on satellite imagery.
* **Zero-Shot Routing vs. Dedicated Meta-Classifiers:** As reported in Appendix A.2.2 of the paper, the ResNet-18 NN-Router achieved a **99.5% domain classification accuracy** across the four domains after dedicated supervised training. In contrast, Gemma-4-12B achieved a zero-shot average domain routing accuracy of **92.76%** (Table 6). Despite receiving zero domain-routing training, ARMDIL maintained performance parity with the supervised NN-Router, staying within 0.40% of its top-1 classification accuracy.
* **Routing Confusion Matrix Analysis:** The Gemma-4-12B routing matrix (Table 4 in the paper) demonstrated high true-positive routing accuracy for facial portraits (**99.82%**), natural objects (**97.80%**), and medical scans (**95.21%**). In contrast, EuroSAT satellite imagery exhibited lower routing confidence (**78.20%** true positive rate, with **12.72%** categorized as `UNSURE`). Perkins et al. observed that texture-uniform aerial imagery often lacks prominent object boundaries, causing MLLM routing hesitation.
* **Ablation Findings (Self-Consistency & Quality Metrics):** Ablation experiments (Tables 6 and 7) evaluated multi-prompt self-consistency (SC) without Chain-of-Thought reasoning. Self-consistency improved EuroSAT routing accuracy (increasing domain accuracy to 82.17%, and 83.87% when quality metrics were omitted) by filtering out sporadic `UNSURE` classifications. Furthermore, omitting image quality metrics proved beneficial for satellite imagery because uniform spatial textures were frequently mischaracterized by heuristic algorithms as lens blur.

### 5. Interpretability & Adaptability: The Safety Dividend

Beyond competitive classification performance, MLLM-driven dynamic routing provides distinct advantages for AI safety, model auditing, and system maintainability.

#### Natural Language Audit Traces

Traditional neural routers and dynamic logit controllers operate as opaque numerical vectors, concealing the internal logic behind expert selection. ARMDIL addresses this opacity by generating human-readable Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning traces for every routing decision. These traces allow safety researchers and red-teamers to inspect visual feature extractions, evaluate heuristic contributions, and audit failure boundaries.

> **Sample MLLM Reasoning Trace (OrganAMNIST Left Kidney Scan):**
> 
> *The user wants me to classify an image into one of five categories: GENERAL, MEDICAL, GEOGRAPHIC, FACIAL, or UNSURE.*
> 1. **Analyze the image:** The image shows a grayscale, cross-sectional view of what appears to be an internal body part. It looks like a CT scan or MRI of a lung or chest area. There are distinct anatomical structures, including what looks like lung tissue (darker areas) and soft tissue/vessels (lighter areas). This fits the definition of MEDICAL imagery (scans, pathology, etc.).
> 2. **Analyze the statistics:** blur score: 0.53 (somewhat blurry, common in medical scans); mean brightness: 0.38 (mid-dark); contrast: 0.20 (high contrast, typical for medical imaging to distinguish tissue); noise estimate: 0.0011 (low noise).
> 3. **Compare with definitions:** GENERAL: Not everyday objects. MEDICAL: Matches (CT/MRI scan). GEOGRAPHIC: Not satellite/aerial. FACIAL: Not a human face. UNSURE: The image is clearly a medical scan.
> 4. **Final Decision:** MEDICAL.

*Safety Takeaway:* Although the MLLM misidentified the specific organ (mistaking a kidney for a lung), its high-level domain reasoning remained robust enough to select the `MEDICAL` domain expert, which subsequently produced the correct downstream organ classification.

#### Prompt-Driven Adaptability

In conventional ensemble architectures, integrating a new image domain or introducing an updated backbone requires retraining meta-classifiers or re-estimating temperature fusion weights across the entire dataset.

ARMDIL eliminates this requirement. To integrate a new task—such as adding a `VEHICLES` category for autonomous driving applications—an operator appends a semantic domain definition to the system prompt and registers the corresponding expert model. This prompt-driven setup enables system expansion without fine-tuning or retraining routing parameters.

### 6. Conclusion & Practical Takeaways

Perkins et al. demonstrate that multimodal LLM agents can orchestrate heterogeneous vision backbones across diverse visual domains. By replacing black-box meta-classifiers with agentic routing, ARMDIL achieves dynamic model selection while providing transparent, human-readable reasoning traces.

#### Actionable Takeaways for AI Safety & Vision System Architects

* **Zero-Shot Routing Performance:** MLLM agentic routing achieves performance parity with specialized, fully trained neural meta-classifiers (achieving a 90.71% global macro F1 vs. 90.41% for an NN-Router) without requiring router training or dataset-specific optimization.
* **Auditable Reasoning Traces:** Chain-of-Thought output traces provide built-in decision transparency, allowing safety practitioners to audit failure boundaries and inspect routing logic.
* **Zero-Retraining System Expansion:** New domain experts and target task categories can be integrated into production pipelines via system prompt updates, bypassing the costs of meta-classifier retraining.
* **Awareness of Heuristic Edge Cases:** Low-level perceptual quality metrics (e.g., blur and contrast scores) can confuse MLLM routers when evaluating domain-uniform textures such as aerial satellite imagery. Prompt configurations should account for these domain-specific characteristics.
* **Scalability:** Future directions identified by the authors include deploying larger commercial MLLMs to enhance reasoning accuracy, refining system prompts, and expanding evaluations to broader multi-domain benchmarks.
