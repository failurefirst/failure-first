### 1. Introduction: Resolving the Latency and Safety Bottlenecks of Embodied LLMs

Autonomous vehicle control architectures face an ongoing technical tension between contextual reasoning capabilities and real-time execution constraints. Large Language Models (LLMs) offer extensive common-sense reasoning and cross-domain generalization, yet **direct deployment of LLMs in low-level vehicle actuation introduces unacceptable inference latency and hallucination vectors**. Conversely, pure Reinforcement Learning (RL) and traditional rule-based controllers deliver high-frequency execution within millisecond deadlines, but **rule-based systems remain brittle under unscripted dynamic conditions, while RL policies lack formal safety guarantees and often degrade when encountering unseen edge cases**.

To resolve this trade-off, Azarafza et al. (2026) propose a hybrid autonomous driving framework that strictly decouples slow, high-level LLM reasoning from high-frequency real-time execution loops. Instead of placing the generative model directly inside the actuation path, the authors utilize LLMs strictly in advisory and offline roles: generating static executable driving rules and iteratively refining low-level RL reward functions. Evaluated within randomized CARLA simulator environments, the architecture constructed by Azarafza et al. demonstrates how structured containment layers can isolate vehicle control from non-deterministic generative failures while maintaining adaptive reasoning capabilities.

### 2. Architectural Partitioning: Isolating LLMs from the Control Loop

Azarafza et al. establish architectural containment by partitioning system capabilities into distinct operational layers. High-level contextual analysis is completely separated from high-frequency vehicle control, ensuring that runtime execution guarantees remain bounded by real-time latency deadlines.

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                            PERCEPTION LAYER                           |
|       YOLOv11 Camera       LiDAR Sensor       Polynomial Lane Fit     |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
                                   |
                                   v
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                           MULTI-AGENT LAYER                           |
|  Safety (ASIL-D) | Situation (ASIL-C) | Planning (ASIL-B) | Behavior (ASIL-A) |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
                                   |
                                   v
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                    PRIORITY FUSION ORCHESTRATOR                       |
|         Priority Weighting Formula  &  Deterministic Stop Veto        |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
                                   |
                                   v
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                        DRIVING MODE & CONTROL                         |
|         Rule-Based PID Controllers  |  Dual PPO Actor-Critic          |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

#### 2.1 Multi-Agent Layer and Priority Fusion Arbiter

The runtime reasoning stack designed by Azarafza et al. relies on four parallel, specialized agents. Each agent is mapped to a specific Automotive Safety Integrity Level (ASIL) defining its authority and operational scope within the vehicle's decision-making hierarchy.

| Agent Name | ASIL Rating | Functional Responsibility & Risk Metrics | Priority Base Weight ($w_{base}$) & ASIL Multiplier ($m_{ASIL}$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Safety Agent** | ASIL-D | Collision risk assessment via Time-To-Collision (TTC) calculation from closing distance/relative velocity thresholds (<1.5s emergency, <3.0s warning, <5.0s caution); common-sense rule evaluation; holistic risk calculation ($R_{holistic}$). | $w_{base} = 2.5$<br>$m_{ASIL} = 2.0$ |
| **Situation Agent** | ASIL-C | Operational Design Domain (ODD) compliance analysis based on speed, weather, lighting, road geometry, sensor health, and traffic density; classifies conditions across four violation states (normal, edge case, degraded, out of ODD). | $w_{base} = 1.5$<br>$m_{ASIL} = 1.5$ |
| **Planning Agent** | ASIL-B | Strategic decision-making, target speed selection, and route/turn planning using distance-weighted scoring with exploration factors and turn history penalties. | $w_{base} = 1.2$<br>$m_{ASIL} = 1.2$ |
| **Behavior Agent** | ASIL-A | Driving quality and passenger comfort optimization; measures smoothness via steering variance calculated across a sliding window of recent lane center offsets. | $w_{base} = 0.8$<br>$m_{ASIL} = 1.0$ |

To combine these parallel recommendations into actionable control commands, the central decision arbitrator evaluates an effective weight $w_i$ for each agent $i$:

$$w_i = w_{base,i} \cdot m_{ASIL,i} \cdot c_i \cdot b_{conflict,i}$$

Where $c_i$ represents the agent's confidence score, and $b_{conflict,i}$ is a dynamic conflict resolution multiplier set to $1.3$ for the agent winning initial conflict resolution and $1.0$ otherwise. 

To determine system safety state, the Safety Agent computes a holistic risk score ($R_{holistic}$) combining perception and environmental factors:

$$R_{holistic} = \min\left(0.5 \cdot R_{collision} + 0.1 \cdot N_{faults} + 0.2 \cdot L_{perception} + R_{traffic} + R_{intersection},\ 1.0\right)$$

Where $N_{faults}$ is the count of sensor faults, $L_{perception}$ is the perception limitation score, $R_{traffic} = 0.15$ during red light states, and $R_{intersection} = 0.1$ when navigating intersections. Furthermore, the framework enforces a deterministic distance-based collision floor: whenever the measured front distance drops below the emergency braking distance, the collision risk is updated as $R_{collision} = \max(R_{collision}, 0.9)$, guaranteeing immediate escalation regardless of relative velocity or calculated TTC.

The calculated risk values map directly to system operating states:
* **Normal Operation**: $R_{holistic} \le 0.4$
* **Degraded Operation**: $0.4 < R_{holistic} \le 0.7$
* **Minimal Risk Condition**: $R_{holistic} > 0.7$
* **Safe Stop Override**: $R_{collision} > 0.9$

Crucially, the orchestrator enforces a **deterministic safety override**: if any ASIL-D (Safety) or ASIL-C (Situation) agent issues a stop recommendation with a confidence score exceeding the predefined system threshold, that agent possesses absolute veto power. This forces an immediate vehicle stop regardless of conflicting recommendations from lower-tier planning or behavior agents.

#### 2.2 Perception and Actuation Infrastructure

The physical runtime pipeline relies on a dedicated perception stack feeding directly into low-level execution controllers:

1. **Perception Stack**:
   * **YOLOv11 Object Detection**: Processes camera imagery to detect and classify dynamic traffic participants (vehicles, pedestrians, cyclists) and signal states (traffic lights) with real-time confidence scores.
   * **LiDAR Sensing**: Provides spatial depth mapping and precise object distance measurements.
   * **Polynomial Lane Detection**: Uses edge detection combined with polynomial fitting to compute lane boundary geometry, vehicle center offsets, and road curvature ($\kappa$).
   * **Intersection Marking Detector**: Identifies horizontal crosswalk markings and evaluates lane width metrics.
2. **Execution Controllers**:
   * **Rule-Based PID Controllers**: Manage deterministic execution baseline logic.
   * **Dual PPO Actor-Critic Networks**: Control is explicitly factorized into two independent Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) neural networks handling longitudinal (speed) and lateral (curve steering) maneuvers, respectively. This structural decoupling avoids monolithic policy brittleness, isolates lateral stabilization dynamics from longitudinal acceleration, and allows the Safety Agent to execute independent control overrides on a single control axis when necessary.

Both RL controllers rely on standardized, normalized input state spaces to maintain numerical stability during inference:

| Controller | Input State Variable | Value / Normalization Scale |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **RL Speed Controller** | Current Vehicle Speed ($v$)<br>Target Speed ($v_{target}$)<br>Front Object Distance ($d$)<br>Front-Left / Front-Right Distance ($d_{fl}, d_{fr}$) | $v / 50$<br>$v_{target} / 50$<br>$d / 50$<br>$d / 30$ |
| **RL Curve Controller** | Center Offset ($o$)<br>Road Curvature ($\kappa$)<br>Current Vehicle Speed ($v$)<br>Front Object Distance ($d$) | $o / 2$<br>$10\kappa$<br>$v / 50$<br>$d / 50$ |

### 3. Dual-Mechanistic LLM Integration: Offline Shaping and Common-Sense Rules

Azarafza et al. integrate generative AI models through a dual-mechanistic approach that completely avoids placing LLM inference steps within the vehicle's real-time actuation loop.

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                              OFFLINE LLM INTEGRATION                              |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                                                                                   |
|  [ Offline RL Reward Refinement ]                [ Executable Rule Extraction ]   |
|                                                                                   |
|    1. CARLA Trajectory Logs (1,000 eps)             1. Claude LLM Reasoner        |
|                       |                                      |                    |
|                       v                                      v                    |
|    2. GPT-5.2 Diagnostic Feedback                   2. ~100 Executable Rules      |
|                       |                                      |                    |
|                       v                                      v                    |
|    3. Refined PPO Reward Scale ($V_0 \rightarrow V_3$)      3. ASIL-D Safety Engine       |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

#### 3.1 Offline RL Reward Refinement via GPT-5.2

To optimize the lateral PPO curve-steering policy, the authors collect trajectory summaries from 1,000 baseline CARLA driving episodes (Version $V_0$). These diagnostic summaries—containing telemetry on steering jitter, center offset deviations, overshoot patterns, and late corrections—are evaluated offline by **GPT-5.2**. 

GPT-5.2 analyzes these failure patterns and outputs recommendations for updating the RL reward parameters. The modifications focus on:
* Strongly penalizing steering oscillations during curve navigation (`is_curve`).
* Introducing steering jerk penalties ($\Delta \text{steer}$) to suppress rapid sign changes and dithering in bends.
* Applying non-linear scaling to lane-centering penalties during curved segments.
* Incorporating explicit curve-anticipation and heading-alignment shaping terms to mitigate overshoot.

These feedback loops undergo human validation prior to policy retraining, resulting in four iterative reward variants ($V_0$ through $V_3$).

#### 3.2 Executable Common-Sense Reasoning Engine via Claude

To provide high-level contextual driving knowledge, Azarafza et al. utilize **Claude** to generate common-sense driving rules covering diverse weather, light, and traffic conditions. Out of more than 1,000 generated rules, approximately 100 executable rules matching CARLA simulator API capabilities were integrated into the runtime Safety Agent.

Key implemented rules and their execution logic include:

* **Rule 53 (Heavy Rain Speed Reduction)**: Triggers major target speed reduction when sensor state registers heavy rain precipitation.
* **Rule 75 (Obscured Visibility at Intersections)**: Reduces vehicle velocity when cross-traffic visibility metrics fall below safe thresholds.
* **Rule 156 / Rule 93 (Storm Following Distance)**: Dynamically expands the mandatory vehicle-following gap upon detecting approaching storm conditions.

> **Executable Rule Logic (Safety Agent Runtime Execution)**
>
> **IF** `perception.weather == "HEAVY_RAIN"`:
> * `agent.set_target_speed_modifier(0.5)`
> * `agent.set_mode("DEFENSIVE")`
> * *(Execution: Rule 53 - Major speed reduction in heavy rain)*
>
> **IF** `perception.cross_traffic_visible == FALSE` **AND** `perception.at_intersection == TRUE`:
> * `agent.set_action("SLOW_DOWN")`
> * `agent.set_mode("CAUTION")`
> * *(Execution: Rule 75 - Speed reduction under obscured cross-traffic visibility)*
>
> **IF** `perception.following_distance < THRESHOLD_STORM` **AND** `perception.weather == "STORM"`:
> * `agent.set_action("INCREASE_GAP")`
> * `agent.set_mode("DEFENSIVE")`
> * *(Execution: Rule 156 & Rule 93 - Storm following distance enforcement)*

### 4. Empirical Evaluation in CARLA Simulations

Azarafza et al. evaluated the four curve-steering policy variants ($V_0$ through $V_3$) across 1,000 randomized episodes in CARLA Town02. Trajectory performance across the final 200 evaluation episodes is summarized in the table below:

| Variant Identifier | Median Lane Error | Steering-Change Magnitude / Oscillation Rate (%) | Median Curve Exposure (Steps per episode) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **V0** (No LLM Guidance) | 0.0780 | 0.3019 / 43.18% | 113.0 |
| **V1** (1st LLM Refinement) | 0.0733 | 0.3412 / 49.18% | 99.0 |
| **V2** (2nd LLM Refinement) | 0.0770 | 0.3708 / 47.61% | 135.5 |
| **V3** (3rd LLM Refinement) | **0.0627** | 0.3119 / 45.27% | **160.0** |

#### Analysis of the "V0 Degenerate Collapse" Anomaly

A superficial analysis of the quantitative logs might suggest that the baseline model ($V_0$) performed well due to its lower steering oscillation rate (43.18%) and low steering-change magnitude (0.3019). However, Azarafza et al. highlight that **V0's apparent stability is a deceptive artifact caused by degenerate trajectory collapse**. 

During training, $V_0$'s curve exposure collapsed to between 21.5 and 113 steps per episode. Because the $V_0$ policy failed to navigate curves successfully, it frequently aborted curve traversal early or failed to enter bends entirely. This drastic reduction in actual curve driving opportunities artificially depressed its steering change and oscillation metrics. 

In contrast, **$V_3$ achieved genuine stabilization**: it maintained the highest sustained curve exposure (160.0 median steps across the last 200 episodes, and 193.5 steps at episode 1,000) while simultaneously delivering the lowest median lane error (0.0627 for the last 200 episodes; 0.0514 at episode 1,000) and reducing steering oscillations compared to intermediate variants $V_1$ and $V_2$.

### 5. Architectural Comparison with Existing LLM-Driving Frameworks

The hybrid framework proposed by Azarafza et al. addresses specific failure modes present in contemporary LLM-centric and RL driving systems:

| System / Framework | Architectural Approach | Primary Mechanism | Failure Mode / Limitation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Direct LLM Control / End-to-End** | Direct high-frequency LLM execution | Generative reasoning directly drives low-level vehicle actuation controls. | Severe latency violations exceeding real-time deadlines; catastrophic hallucination risks during emergency maneuvers. |
| **HCRMP** (Chen et al., 2025) | LLM-Hinted Contextual RL | LLM generates semantic state hints; bridges low/high frequency via memory caching. | Performance degrades during dynamic traffic density shifts; lacks validation outside idealized CARLA conditions. |
| **Li et al.** (2025) | Hierarchical LLM-RL Architecture | LLM generates meta-actions/goals; low-level RL executes via Goal Gradient Transfer. | Goal generation remains vulnerable to unsafe hallucinations in edge cases; limited evaluation pool. |
| **Choi & Kim** (2025) | Safety Potential RL with Pure Pursuit | Dense intrinsic risk shaping via path overlap and temporal weighting. | Restricted to single-dimensional longitudinal control; relies on ground-truth simulator states and rule-based Pure Pursuit steering; underpredicts risk during diagonal interactions. |
| **LSADQN** (Ren & Xing, 2026) | Contrastive Value Learning with Online Screening | Physical filters (TTC) invoke GPT-4 online for ambiguous samples to regularize value learning. | Online LLM invocation introduces computational overhead and latency bottlenecks; heuristic safety thresholds lack formal guarantees. |
| **Azarafza et al.** (Target Paper) | Multi-Agent Orchestration with Decoupled LLM | Multi-agent priority arbiter with deterministic ASIL vetoes, offline LLM reward shaping (GPT-5.2), and static rule extraction (Claude). | Validation restricted to CARLA simulator environment; agent weights require domain-specific tuning for non-urban driving (e.g., highways). |

### 6. Key Takeaways for Information Architecture & AI Safety

1. **Architectural Decoupling as Safety Containment**: Isolating generative models to offline reward optimization and advisory rule synthesis isolates real-time control loops from inference latency spikes and non-deterministic hallucination vectors.
2. **Priority-Weighted Deterministic Vetoes**: Combining learned RL controllers with an ASIL-rated priority arbiter ensures bounded runtime behavior, guaranteeing that hard deterministic safety overrides (ASIL-D/C) take precedence over policy predictions.
3. **Iterative LLM Reward Shaping**: Utilizing LLMs offline to diagnose trajectory failure logs bridges the gap between high-level common-sense requirements and low-level mathematical reward formulations, effectively resolving policy instability without adding runtime computational overhead.
